Composition of air|Dry air is a mixture dominated by nitrogen and oxygen, with smaller amounts of argon and carbon dioxide.|Volume fractions describe how much of a gas mixture each component occupies at the same conditions. Water vapour varies with humidity, so dry-air percentages exclude it.|In 100 L of dry air, approximately 78 L is nitrogen and 21 L is oxygen. The remaining roughly 1 L contains mainly argon and trace gases; the components are mixed, not chemically bonded together.|gas volume fraction = component volume / total volume|Oxygen is not the most abundant gas in air; nitrogen is. Water-vapour content is not a fixed dry-air percentage.|Air separation supplies nitrogen for inert packaging and oxygen for industrial processes.
Water cycle and purification|Evaporation, condensation and precipitation move water between reservoirs; purification removes particular contaminants.|Filtration traps suspended particles but ordinarily does not remove dissolved salts. Distillation vaporizes water and condenses the vapour, leaving most nonvolatile solutes behind. Disinfection addresses microorganisms rather than salt content.|A sand-water-salt mixture passes through a filter: sand remains on the paper, but salt remains in the filtrate. Distilling the filtrate gives a much less salty condensate because dissolved salt does not vaporize with the water.|filtration separates suspended solids; distillation separates by volatility|Clear filtered water is not necessarily free of dissolved chemicals or microorganisms.|Water treatment combines several operations because turbidity, dissolved solutes and microbial contamination require different controls.
Greenhouse effect|Greenhouse gases absorb and emit infrared radiation and alter the rate at which Earth loses energy to space.|Sunlight warms Earth's surface. The surface emits infrared radiation, some of which is absorbed by gases such as carbon dioxide and water vapour. The atmosphere also emits radiation upward and downward; it does not simply prevent all heat from escaping.|Compare an atmosphere containing infrared-active CO2 with one without it while keeping incoming sunlight fixed. Absorption and re-emission of outgoing infrared change the energy balance, so the equilibrium surface temperature differs.|absorbed solar energy = outgoing energy at thermal equilibrium|Do not describe the greenhouse effect as the ozone hole or as oxygen stopping all heat from escaping.|Infrared spectra and atmospheric composition help quantify the radiative effects of changing greenhouse-gas concentrations.
Resources and sustainability|Sustainable use of resources considers extraction, lifetime, recovery and waste rather than only purchase cost.|Finite mineral deposits and fossil fuels are replenished much more slowly than they are consumed. Reuse preserves an object, while recycling recovers material and may still require energy and processing.|Compare using a refillable glass container 20 times with replacing 20 single-use containers. Count washing, transport and manufacturing inputs before concluding which option has lower total environmental impact.|resource demand = virgin input + processing demand - recovered material|A recyclable label does not prove that an item is collected or recycled in practice.|Material-flow accounting helps plan collection systems and reduce demand for virgin resources.
Reactivity of metals|A more reactive metal can displace ions of a less reactive metal from a suitable aqueous solution.|Metal reactivity concerns the tendency to lose electrons under specified conditions. An observed displacement couples metal oxidation to reduction of another metal ion.|Place zinc in copper sulfate solution: Zn + Cu2+ -> Zn2+ + Cu. Copper deposits as zinc dissolves. Copper placed in zinc sulfate does not cause the reverse displacement under ordinary conditions.|Zn(s) + Cu2+(aq) -> Zn2+(aq) + Cu(s)|A metal does not displace every other metal merely because both conduct electricity.|Displacement reactions underpin cementation processes used to recover dissolved metals.
Carbon forms and fuels|Carbon allotropes have different bonding networks, while carbon-containing fuels release energy on oxidation.|Diamond has a three-dimensional covalent network; graphite contains strongly bonded sheets with mobile electrons. Combustion changes chemical composition and is distinct from changing allotrope.|Graphite conducts along its sheets, whereas pure diamond is an electrical insulator under ordinary conditions. Burning carbon completely gives CO2; limited oxygen can also produce poisonous CO.|C + O2 -> CO2|Diamond and graphite differ in structure, not in the identity of their carbon atoms. Incomplete combustion is not guaranteed to form only CO2.|Graphite electrodes and diamond cutting tools exploit contrasting structures; ventilation matters when burning carbon fuels.
Laws of chemical combination|Conservation of mass and definite proportions constrain how elements combine in compounds.|A balanced reaction preserves each element's atoms. A pure compound contains its constituent elements in a characteristic mass ratio, although mixtures can have variable composition.|When 2 g of hydrogen reacts completely with 16 g of oxygen, 18 g of water forms in a closed system. Doubling both masses gives 36 g of water, preserving the 1:8 hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio.|mass of reactants = mass of products in a closed system|Apparent mass loss from an open beaker may be escaping gas rather than destroyed matter.|Mass balances check reaction equations and quantify material flows through chemical plants.
Percentage composition|Mass percentage states what fraction of a compound's mass comes from one element.|Multiply each atomic mass by its subscript, add the contributions to obtain formula mass, and divide the selected element's contribution by the total. Percentages for all elements sum to 100 within rounding.|Using H = 1 and O = 16, water has formula mass 18. Hydrogen contributes 2/18 x 100 = 11.1%, while oxygen contributes 16/18 x 100 = 88.9%.|element mass percent = element mass contribution / formula mass x 100|Use the mass of all atoms of the element, not just one atom when its subscript exceeds one.|Elemental-analysis percentages help infer empirical formulae and assess sample composition.
Activity series|The activity series orders metals by their tendency to undergo oxidation in common displacement reactions.|Relative position predicts whether a metal can reduce another metal ion. Reaction rate, surface films and solution conditions can affect what is observed, so the series is not a universal speed ranking.|Iron added to copper sulfate deposits copper: Fe + Cu2+ -> Fe2+ + Cu. Silver added to iron sulfate does not displace iron because silver is less readily oxidized in this comparison.|more active metal + less active metal ion -> displacement|Do not infer that an unreactive-looking aluminium surface means aluminium is intrinsically less active; its oxide film can passivate it.|The series guides sacrificial-anode selection and elementary metal-displacement predictions.
Occurrence of metals|A metal's natural occurrence depends partly on its reactivity and the stability of its compounds.|Very reactive metals usually occur as compounds, while some less reactive metals can occur in native form. A mineral becomes an ore when extraction is economically and technically worthwhile.|Sodium is found in salts rather than as lumps of native sodium. Gold can occur as native metal. Bauxite is an important aluminium ore, but not every aluminium-containing rock is an economical ore.|ore = mineral deposit suitable for economical extraction|Mineral and ore are not interchangeable terms, and native occurrence does not imply unlimited abundance.|Ore assessment connects chemical composition with extraction route, energy demand and recoverable metal content.
Ore concentration|Ore concentration increases the fraction of useful mineral before chemical extraction.|Separation methods exploit differences such as density, magnetic behaviour or surface wettability. The chosen process must match the mineral rather than merely the metal's name.|In froth flotation of a suitable sulfide ore, treated mineral particles attach to air bubbles and enter the froth, while much of the wetted gangue remains in the slurry.|concentration separates valuable mineral from gangue|Flotation does not reduce metal ions to metal; concentration and reduction are different stages.|Concentration reduces the mass of gangue that must be transported, heated or chemically treated.
Alloys|An alloy is a metallic material containing more than one element and designed for useful properties.|Substitutional atoms replace host atoms; small interstitial atoms can occupy spaces in a metallic lattice. These changes can alter strength, corrosion behaviour and electrical conductivity.|Compare pure iron with a low-carbon steel. A small carbon content changes the microstructure and mechanical response; steel is not simply iron coated with carbon. Brass contains copper and zinc.|composition + microstructure -> alloy properties|An alloy need not be a compound with a fixed formula, and not every alloy is a single solid phase.|Steel structures, brass fittings and corrosion-resistant alloys are selected by matching composition and processing to service conditions.
Recycling metals|Metal recycling recovers useful metal from scrap through sorting, separation and reprocessing.|Recycling can avoid some primary ore processing but still uses energy. Contamination and alloy mixing can limit the quality of recovered material.|A mixed stream of aluminium beverage cans and steel cans is first separated using differences including magnetism. Aluminium scrap is then prepared for remelting; steel contamination must not be treated as part of the desired aluminium alloy.|recoverable metal = scrap mass x metal fraction x recovery yield|Recycling is not energy-free, and mixing incompatible alloys can reduce the value of the recovered metal.|Closed-loop scrap collection helps preserve alloy quality and reduce primary metal demand.
Homologous series|A homologous series contains compounds with the same functional group and a common general formula, with successive members differing by CH2.|Similar functional groups produce related reaction patterns. Physical properties such as boiling point often vary progressively with chain length, but structural isomers can complicate simple comparisons.|Methanol CH3OH and ethanol C2H5OH are consecutive members of the monohydric alcohol series. Their formulas differ by CH2, corresponding to about 14 g/mol, and both contain an OH group.|successive homologues differ by CH2|Compounds are not homologues merely because their formulas contain carbon; the functional group and series must match.|Homologous trends help compare solvents and predict changes in volatility across a related family.
Nuclear stability|Nuclear stability depends on neutron-to-proton balance, pairing and binding energy rather than ordinary chemical bonding.|Nuclides outside stable combinations can undergo radioactive transformation. A larger neutron number does not automatically make a nucleus more stable, and chemical oxidation state is not a nuclear stability test.|Carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons and is stable. Carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons and undergoes beta-minus decay to nitrogen-14, conserving nucleon number and charge when the emitted particles are included.|14C -> 14N + beta- + antineutrino|Isotopes of the same element can have very different nuclear stability despite similar chemical behaviour.|Radiocarbon dating uses the decay of carbon-14 rather than any change in ordinary carbon bonding.
Nuclear equations|A nuclear equation tracks changes in nuclides and emitted particles while conserving mass number and charge.|Write both mass number A and atomic number Z for every nuclear species. An alpha particle contributes A = 4 and Z = 2, while a beta-minus electron contributes A = 0 and Z = -1.|For 238U alpha decay, subtract four from 238 and two from 92 to obtain 234Th. The equation is 238U -> 234Th + 4He, with 238 = 234 + 4 and 92 = 90 + 2.|sum of A and sum of Z are conserved|Do not balance a nuclear equation using only element symbols; nucleon number and charge must both balance.|Balanced decay equations identify daughter nuclides and support interpretation of decay chains.
Mass defect|Mass defect is the difference between the summed masses of separated nucleons and the bound nucleus.|The missing rest mass corresponds to nuclear binding energy. Nuclear masses and atomic masses require consistent accounting for electrons when used in a calculation.|A nucleus with a mass defect of 0.0100 u has a binding energy of 0.0100 x 931.5 = 9.315 MeV. The positive binding energy is the energy required to separate that nucleus into its nucleons.|binding energy = mass defect x c^2|A mass defect is not an error in the balance, and u must be converted consistently before using SI energy units.|Mass measurements permit quantitative comparisons of nuclear binding and reaction energy release.
Binding energy|Binding energy is the energy required to separate a bound nucleus into free protons and neutrons.|Binding energy per nucleon allows comparison between nuclei of different sizes. A high total binding energy alone does not mean a nucleus has the highest binding energy per nucleon.|If a nucleus with 12 nucleons has total binding energy 92 MeV, its binding energy per nucleon is 92/12 = 7.67 MeV. Compare that quotient rather than 92 MeV alone with another nucleus.|binding energy per nucleon = total binding energy / A|Do not confuse total binding energy with binding energy per nucleon when comparing nuclear stability.|Binding-energy comparisons explain why certain fission and fusion reactions release energy.
Equivalent mass|Equivalent mass is molar mass divided by the reaction-specific number of transferable charges or reacting equivalents.|The n-factor depends on the stated reaction. An acid's equivalent mass cannot be assigned without specifying how many protons react in that process.|For complete neutralisation of both protons of H2SO4, equivalent mass = 98/2 = 49 g per equivalent. If only one proton is neutralised in the stated reaction, the corresponding value is 98 g per equivalent.|equivalent mass = molar mass / reaction n-factor|The n-factor is not always the number of hydrogen atoms written in a formula.|Reaction-specific equivalents simplify stoichiometric comparison in acid-base and redox titration calculations.
Photoelectric effect|The photoelectric effect ejects electrons from a material when photon energy exceeds its work function.|Increasing intensity supplies more photons but does not compensate for photon energy below threshold. Above threshold, the maximum electron kinetic energy depends on frequency.|If a photon carries 5.0 eV and the work function is 2.0 eV, the maximum photoelectron kinetic energy is 3.0 eV. A 1.5 eV photon cannot eject an electron from that surface regardless of beam intensity in this model.|Kmax = h nu - work function|Brightness is not the same as photon frequency, and threshold energy must be exceeded.|Photoelectron measurements connect electronic binding energies with incident photon energies.
de Broglie relation|A particle with momentum p has de Broglie wavelength h/p.|Matter-wave wavelength decreases as momentum increases. The nonrelativistic substitution p = mv is valid only when the particle speed is sufficiently below the speed of light.|If an electron's momentum doubles, its de Broglie wavelength halves because h is constant. A particle with p = 6.626 x 10^-24 kg m/s has wavelength 1.00 x 10^-10 m.|lambda = h/p|Do not use a light-wave speed in p = mv for an electron, or confuse momentum with kinetic energy.|Electron diffraction uses matter-wave wavelengths comparable to atomic spacings.
Heisenberg uncertainty|Position and momentum cannot simultaneously have arbitrarily narrow distributions in a quantum state.|The relation concerns intrinsic quantum spreads, not merely poor measuring instruments. Position-momentum uncertainty must be distinguished from uncertainty in macroscopic instrument readings.|An electron localized to a smaller spatial region requires a broader spread of momentum components. Halving a position uncertainty requires the lower bound on momentum uncertainty to double.|Delta x Delta p >= hbar/2|An improved microscope does not remove the intrinsic position-momentum uncertainty relation.|Quantum confinement links localization of electrons to increased kinetic-energy scales.
Bond order and magnetism|Molecular orbital occupancy determines bond order and whether a molecule has unpaired electrons.|Bond order counts the difference between bonding and antibonding electrons divided by two. Paramagnetism requires unpaired electrons, not simply an odd atomic number somewhere in a formula.|In O2, two electrons occupy separate degenerate pi-star orbitals. The molecular orbital count gives bond order 2 and two unpaired electrons, so O2 is paramagnetic despite having a double bond.|bond order = (bonding electrons - antibonding electrons)/2|A Lewis double-bond drawing by itself does not describe the observed magnetic behaviour of O2.|Magnetic measurements help test molecular orbital descriptions of small molecules.
Fajans rules|A small highly charged cation can polarize a large polarizable anion and increase covalent character.|Polarizing power increases with cation charge density, while anion polarizability tends to increase with size. These are qualitative trends rather than an equation assigning an exact covalent percentage.|Comparing anhydrous AlCl3 with NaCl, Al3+ has much greater polarizing power than Na+. Greater distortion of chloride electron density helps explain the stronger covalent character of AlCl3.|small highly charged cation + polarizable anion -> increased covalent character|Formal ionic charges do not guarantee purely ionic bonding, and the rules do not predict an exact melting point.|Polarization arguments help explain bonding trends across metal halides.
Diagonal relationships|Certain diagonally adjacent elements in the periodic table show similarities because opposing size and charge trends partly compensate.|Lithium and magnesium are a common comparison. Similar charge-density effects can produce similarities in compound behaviour even though the elements belong to different groups.|Lithium carbonate and magnesium carbonate decompose on heating, unlike the more thermally stable carbonates of the heavier alkali metals under comparable conditions. This is one example of a diagonal relationship, not identical chemistry in every reaction.|diagonal similarity reflects competing size and charge trends|A diagonal relationship does not place lithium and magnesium in the same group or give them identical valence electrons.|Comparing related compounds reveals where simple group trends require qualification.
Important compounds|The uses of sodium and calcium compounds depend on the identities of the ions and the chemical reactions they undergo.|Sodium carbonate is a basic salt used in glass manufacture; calcium carbonate decomposes to calcium oxide on strong heating. Calcium hydroxide reacts with carbon dioxide to form a carbonate precipitate.|Passing CO2 into limewater forms white CaCO3: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 -> CaCO3 + H2O. Excess CO2 can dissolve the precipitate by forming soluble hydrogencarbonate species.|Ca(OH)2 + CO2 -> CaCO3 + H2O|Calcium carbonate, calcium oxide and calcium hydroxide are different substances and are not interchangeable formulas.|Limestone and lime chemistry connect construction materials, water treatment and industrial processing.
Groups 13 and 14|Groups 13 and 14 show changes in bonding and oxidation-state stability down each group.|Boron is commonly electron-deficient in its compounds; carbon forms strong multiple bonds. The relative stability of lower oxidation states often increases down the heavier p-block elements because the ns2 pair participates less readily.|Compare carbon dioxide with lead(II) oxide: carbon commonly displays +4 in CO2, while +2 is especially important for lead. The comparison illustrates an oxidation-state trend rather than a rule that every group-14 compound has the same oxidation state.|group 13: ns2 np1; group 14: ns2 np2|The group number does not force one oxidation state in every compound.|Boron compounds, aluminium materials and silicon-based materials illustrate diverse p-block bonding.
Groups 15 and 16|Groups 15 and 16 include elements with changing oxidation states, hydride behaviour and multiple-bonding ability.|Nitrogen and oxygen differ from their heavier congeners because of small size and strong bonding effects. Hydride trends reflect both bond strength and intermolecular interactions.|Compare H2O and H2S: water's extensive hydrogen bonding raises its boiling point unusually high relative to the heavier group-16 hydrides. The trend cannot be explained by molar mass alone.|group 15: ns2 np3; group 16: ns2 np4|Do not apply a simple increasing-mass boiling-point rule without considering hydrogen bonding.|Hydrides and oxides of these groups are central to fertilizer chemistry, acids and environmental processes.
Halogens|Halogens are reactive group-17 elements whose atoms commonly gain one electron or share one covalent bond.|Their elemental oxidizing power generally decreases down the group under comparable aqueous conditions. Halide ions have different properties from the corresponding elemental halogens.|Chlorine added to aqueous bromide oxidizes Br- to Br2: Cl2 + 2Br- -> 2Cl- + Br2. Bromine does not similarly displace chloride under ordinary conditions.|halogen atom + electron -> halide ion|Chlorine gas and chloride ions are not the same material and do not have the same hazards.|Halogen displacement reactions help compare oxidizing strength and identify halide chemistry.
Noble gases|Noble gases have filled valence shells and generally low chemical reactivity, although heavier members can form compounds.|Closed-shell configurations make electron removal difficult. Xenon compounds show that chemical inertness is relative, especially with strongly oxidizing reactants.|XeF2 is a real xenon compound with a linear molecular shape. Its existence disproves the absolute claim that noble gases can never form chemical bonds.|filled valence shell usually reduces reactivity|Do not interpret noble-gas classification as proof that all noble gases are chemically incapable of forming compounds.|Noble gases provide inert atmospheres and specialized lighting environments.
Magnetic properties|Unpaired electrons contribute to paramagnetism, while paired-electron contributions are generally diamagnetic.|For a simple first-row transition-metal ion with largely quenched orbital angular momentum, the spin-only estimate depends on the number n of unpaired electrons. More complete treatments may require orbital and exchange effects.|High-spin Fe3+ is d5 with five unpaired electrons. Its spin-only magnetic moment is sqrt(5 x 7) = 5.92 Bohr magnetons, whereas a low-spin d6 ion has no unpaired electrons in an octahedral field.|mu spin-only = sqrt(n(n+2)) Bohr magnetons|Oxidation state alone does not determine spin state; ligand field and geometry also matter.|Magnetic susceptibility measurements help distinguish possible electronic configurations of metal complexes.
Lanthanoid contraction|Lanthanoid contraction is the progressive decrease in lanthanoid ionic size associated with increasing nuclear charge and incomplete 4f shielding.|The size trend affects later transition elements and makes some pairs chemically similar. It is not caused by removal of a whole electron shell across the series.|Zirconium and hafnium have unusually similar radii and chemistry because the intervening lanthanoids contract. Their separation is therefore more difficult than a simple period-based size argument would suggest.|increasing effective nuclear attraction contracts the 4f series|Do not predict that hafnium must be much larger than zirconium solely because it is one period lower.|The contraction explains similarities that complicate separation of lanthanoids and zirconium-hafnium mixtures.
Actinoids|Actinoids involve filling of 5f-derived states and often show several accessible oxidation states.|All actinoid elements are radioactive, but their half-lives, chemical behaviour and practical handling requirements differ greatly. The 5f orbitals can participate in bonding more extensively than a simple inner-core picture suggests.|Uranium occurs in both +4 and +6 oxidation states; the uranyl ion UO2(2+) contains uranium(VI). Changing oxidation state can change solubility and therefore the design of a separation process.|actinoid chemistry depends strongly on oxidation state and ligand environment|Radioactivity does not imply that all actinoids have identical chemistry or identical decay rates.|Actinoid redox and coordination chemistry are important in environmental transport and specialized nuclear-material separations.
Werner theory|Werner distinguished a metal's primary valence from its secondary valence to explain coordination compounds.|Primary valence corresponds to oxidation state, while secondary valence corresponds to coordination number and directed ligand positions. Counterions lie outside the coordination sphere.|[Co(NH3)6]Cl3 contains a cobalt(III) centre coordinated by six neutral ammonia ligands and three external chloride counterions. Ionization yields one complex cation and three chloride ions.|oxidation state differs from coordination number|Do not count external chloride counterions as ligands merely because they appear in the formula.|Werner's distinction explains conductivity and precipitation evidence for coordination-sphere formulations.
Ligands and nomenclature|Ligands donate electron pairs to a central atom or ion, and nomenclature identifies ligand identities and the metal's oxidation state.|Denticity counts donor atoms from one ligand that bind to the metal. Coordination number counts donor atoms attached overall, not the number of separate ligand molecules.|In [Co(en)3]3+, each ethane-1,2-diamine ligand binds through two nitrogen atoms. Three ligands therefore give coordination number six; neutral en leaves cobalt at oxidation state +3.|coordination number = total bound donor atoms|Three bidentate ligands do not imply coordination number three.|Clear complex names and denticity assignments support comparison of structures and metal-binding agents.
Isomerism|Coordination compounds can share a formula but differ in ligand connectivity or spatial arrangement.|Geometrical isomerism concerns positions such as cis and trans; optical isomerism concerns non-superimposable mirror images. Linkage isomers involve ambidentate ligands attaching through different atoms.|Square-planar [Pt(NH3)2Cl2] has cis and trans forms. In the cis form the chloride ligands occupy adjacent positions, whereas in the trans form they are opposite.|same composition can allow different coordination arrangements|Cis and trans compounds have the same formula but need not have the same reactivity or biological effects.|Structure-dependent activity makes isomer identification important in coordination and medicinal chemistry.
Stability and applications|Formation constants describe thermodynamic preference for a metal-ligand complex under specified conditions.|Chelating ligands can form especially favourable complexes, but formation depends on competing equilibria such as ligand protonation. Thermodynamic stability must be distinguished from substitution rate.|EDTA binds many metal ions in a 1:1 metal-to-ligand ratio. In a calcium titration, pH is controlled because EDTA protonation changes the effective binding strength and indicator response.|formation constant = complex activity / reactant activities|A thermodynamically stable complex is not necessarily slow to undergo ligand exchange.|Complex formation supports metal-ion titrations, selective separations and chelation-based applications.
Electronic effects|Inductive, resonance and hyperconjugative effects redistribute electron density and influence organic reactivity.|The inductive effect is transmitted through sigma bonds, while resonance requires an appropriate conjugated orbital arrangement. These mechanisms should not be used as interchangeable labels.|The chloroacetate ion is more stabilized than acetate by the electron-withdrawing effect of chlorine through sigma bonds, helping explain why chloroacetic acid is stronger than acetic acid.|electron withdrawal can stabilize a nearby conjugate base|Do not draw resonance through an intervening saturated atom that breaks conjugation.|Electronic effects help compare acid strengths, carbocation stability and substituent-directed reactions.
Carbohydrates|Carbohydrates include polyhydroxy aldehydes, ketones and substances that yield such units on hydrolysis.|Monosaccharides can form cyclic hemiacetals or hemiketals. Glycosidic bonding links sugar units, and the availability of an anomeric centre influences reducing behaviour.|Glucose commonly exists as cyclic forms in solution, yet an equilibrium small open-chain fraction allows reducing behaviour. Sucrose joins both anomeric centres in its glycosidic bond and is nonreducing under the usual test conditions.|monosaccharides join through glycosidic bonds|A sweet taste does not determine whether a sugar is reducing, and a cyclic drawing does not imply that glucose cannot open.|Carbohydrate structure influences food properties, digestion and biological energy storage.
Nucleic acids|DNA and RNA are nucleotide polymers whose sequences store or transmit biological information.|Each nucleotide contains a sugar, phosphate and nitrogenous base. Phosphodiester bonds join the backbone, while complementary base pairing is mediated by noncovalent interactions.|In a DNA duplex, a sequence 5'-ATGC-3' pairs antiparallel with 3'-TACG-5'. A-T and G-C pairing does not mean that phosphate groups form the base-pair hydrogen bonds.|nucleotides form sugar-phosphate backbones with directional sequences|Do not confuse the covalent bonds along a strand with the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases.|Sequence complementarity underlies DNA replication and many molecular-recognition methods.
Integrated rate laws|Integrated rate laws relate reactant concentration to time for a specified kinetic model.|A first-order process gives a straight line when ln concentration is plotted against time. Zero- and second-order models require different plots, so a straight raw concentration-time graph does not establish first order.|For a first-order reactant that falls from 0.100 M to 0.050 M in 20.0 s, k = ln(2)/20.0 = 0.0347 s^-1. After another 20.0 s the model predicts 0.025 M.|ln([A]t/[A]0) = -kt for first order|Do not infer reaction order from stoichiometric coefficients unless the reaction is an elementary step.|Time-course measurements can estimate rate constants and shelf-life under conditions where the kinetic model applies.
Complex reactions|Complex reactions involve multiple elementary steps whose intermediates and branching determine the observed rate law.|Consecutive and parallel pathways differ: a consecutive intermediate forms and is consumed, while parallel pathways compete for a starting material. An observed overall equation does not disclose all steps.|For A -> B -> C, B may rise initially and later fall as it is converted to C. A declining A signal alone cannot establish whether B is a stable product or a transient intermediate.|d[B]/dt = k1[A] - k2[B] for consecutive first-order steps|Do not assign the overall stoichiometric equation as a single elementary collision without mechanistic evidence.|Time-resolved profiles reveal intermediates and guide selectivity control in competing reaction networks.
Steady-state approximation|The steady-state approximation treats an intermediate's formation and consumption rates as nearly equal over an appropriate time interval.|The intermediate concentration can remain small even when material flows rapidly through it. This approximation differs from equilibrium: individual forward and reverse elementary steps need not balance.|For A -> I and I -> P with rates k1[A] and k2[I], setting d[I]/dt approximately zero gives [I] approximately k1[A]/k2. Product formation then proceeds at approximately k1[A].|d[I]/dt approximately 0 in the steady-state interval|Do not set the intermediate concentration to zero or confuse steady state with chemical equilibrium.|The approximation converts multistep mechanisms into testable rate expressions.
Transition-state theory|Transition-state theory relates reaction rates to the free-energy barrier between reactants and an activated configuration.|The activated configuration lies near a dividing surface along the reaction coordinate. Activation free energy includes enthalpic and entropic contributions and is distinct from overall reaction free energy.|At the same temperature and prefactor, lowering an activation free-energy barrier by RT ln(10) increases the predicted rate constant tenfold. A favourable product free energy alone does not specify the reaction speed.|k = (kB T/h) exp(-Delta G double-dagger/RT)|An exergonic reaction need not be fast; barrier height and overall free-energy change describe different questions.|Activation parameters help interpret temperature-dependent rates and catalytic acceleration.
Photochemical kinetics|Photochemical rates depend on light absorption and excited-state reaction and decay pathways.|Incident photon flux differs from absorbed photon flux. Excited molecules may react, fluoresce, undergo intersystem crossing or relax without reaction, so light intensity alone cannot predict product formation.|If a sample absorbs 2.0 micromoles of photons per second and forms 0.50 micromoles of product per second, the product quantum yield is 0.25 under those conditions.|product formation rate = quantum yield x absorbed photon rate|Use absorbed rather than incident photons when calculating a quantum yield.|Actinometry and controlled illumination allow comparison of photochemical reaction efficiencies.
Operators and wavefunctions|Quantum observables are represented by operators acting on wavefunctions.|A normalized wavefunction yields probabilities through its squared modulus. An eigenfunction of an operator has a definite corresponding measurement value, while a general superposition need not.|For an energy eigenfunction psi_n, applying the Hamiltonian returns E_n psi_n. Multiplying a normalized wavefunction by a constant phase changes neither its probability density nor observable predictions.|H psi_n = E_n psi_n; integral abs(psi)^2 d tau = 1|A wavefunction amplitude can be negative or complex; probability density cannot be negative.|Operator methods determine allowed energies and expectation values in molecular quantum models.
Particle in a box|An ideal particle confined by infinite walls has discrete translational energy levels.|Boundary conditions require the wavefunction to vanish at the walls. The model has a nonzero ground-state energy and increasingly separated levels, and its limitations matter for real conjugated molecules.|For an electron in a one-dimensional box, doubling box length reduces every energy level to one quarter because E_n is proportional to 1/L^2. The ground state uses n = 1, not n = 0.|E_n = n^2 h^2/(8mL^2), n = 1,2,3,...|Do not allow n = 0 as a stationary state of the infinite-well model.|The model introduces quantization and gives a first approximation to delocalized-electron excitation trends.
Rigid rotor|The rigid-rotor model quantizes molecular rotational energy while treating bond lengths as fixed.|For a diatomic molecule, rotational spacing depends on the moment of inertia, which depends on reduced mass and bond length. Pure rotational electric-dipole spectra require a permanent dipole.|Replacing H with D in a polar diatomic molecule increases reduced mass and the moment of inertia. The rotational constant decreases, so adjacent rotational lines become more closely spaced in the rigid-rotor approximation.|E_J = hbar^2 J(J+1)/(2I)|A homonuclear diatomic molecule does not gain a permanent dipole merely because it rotates.|Rotational spectra provide precise information about molecular moments of inertia and bond lengths.
Harmonic oscillator|The harmonic oscillator models vibration near a potential-energy minimum using a quadratic restoring potential.|Its energy levels are equally spaced and include zero-point energy. Real molecular vibrations become anharmonic at larger displacement, and vibrational IR activity also requires changing dipole moment.|If a bond's force constant stays fixed while reduced mass increases by a factor of four, the harmonic frequency falls by a factor of two. Isotopic substitution therefore shifts vibrational bands without changing the element's bonding framework.|nu = (1/2pi) sqrt(k/mu)|The harmonic ground state does not have zero vibrational energy.|Isotope shifts and normal-mode frequencies help assign molecular vibrations.
Hydrogen atom|The hydrogenic atom provides exact one-electron orbital solutions for a Coulomb potential.|Energy depends on principal quantum number in the nonrelativistic ideal model, while angular and radial factors determine orbital probability distributions. Many-electron atoms introduce electron-electron interactions absent from this model.|A hydrogen electron moving from n = 3 to n = 2 emits a photon corresponding to the energy difference between those levels. The orbital is a probability amplitude, not a small planet travelling along a fixed circular path.|E_n = -13.6 eV/n^2 for hydrogen|Do not transfer the simple hydrogen energy formula unchanged to neutral many-electron atoms.|Hydrogen spectra test quantized energy differences and provide a starting point for orbital theory.
Rotational spectroscopy|Rotational spectroscopy measures transitions between quantized molecular rotational states.|For a simple linear rigid rotor, allowed electric-dipole transitions have Delta J = plus or minus one and require a permanent dipole. Centrifugal distortion causes departures from ideal equal line spacing.|CO has a permanent dipole and displays a pure rotational spectrum. N2 lacks a permanent dipole and does not show the corresponding ordinary electric-dipole rotational spectrum, even though N2 molecules rotate.|rigid linear rotor line spacing approximately 2B|Absence of a pure rotational electric-dipole spectrum does not mean a molecule has no rotational motion.|Microwave spectra identify gas-phase molecules and constrain molecular geometry.
Infrared and Raman spectroscopy|Infrared and Raman selection rules probe different changes during a molecular vibration.|IR activity requires a changing dipole moment; Raman activity requires a changing polarizability. These are response properties, not simply labels for bond polarity.|The symmetric stretch of CO2 is Raman-active but IR-inactive in the ideal centrosymmetric molecule. Its asymmetric stretch changes the dipole moment and is IR-active.|IR: changing dipole; Raman: changing polarizability|A vibration's IR inactivity does not imply that it cannot be observed by another spectroscopic method.|Combining IR and Raman spectra provides complementary vibrational assignments.
UV-visible spectroscopy|UV-visible spectroscopy measures electronic absorption and can quantify concentration under suitable conditions.|Absorbance depends on concentration, path length and wavelength-dependent molar absorptivity. Chemical equilibria, scattering and high-concentration effects can invalidate a simple linear calibration.|At a selected wavelength, epsilon = 10000 L mol^-1 cm^-1 and a 1.00 cm path give A = 0.500 for c = 5.00 x 10^-5 mol/L. The name hydride does not identify one universal reaction type.|NaH reacts with water to form sodium hydroxide and hydrogen: NaH + H2O -> NaOH + H2. This reflects hydride-ion protonation, not a hydrogen-bonding interaction between two neutral molecules.|H- + H2O -> H2 + OH-|Do not confuse hydride bonding with intermolecular hydrogen bonding.|Hydrides are used as reagents and as components of hydrogen-related materials.
Ligand field theory|Ligand field theory describes metal-ligand electronic structure using orbital interactions as well as symmetry and electron repulsion.|Unlike a purely electrostatic crystal-field picture, ligand-field treatments can include covalent mixing, sigma donation and pi effects. Spectra and magnetic data constrain the description.|A pi-acceptor ligand such as CO can increase relevant orbital energy separations through metal-ligand interactions. Target identification and validation|Target identification proposes a biological component to modulate; target validation tests whether changing it can cause the desired biological effect.|Association with disease is not sufficient evidence of causation or druggability. Orthogonal genetic and chemical perturbations can reveal target dependence and off-target explanations.|If suppressing a protein genetically and inhibiting it with structurally different selective compounds produce a consistent phenotype, confidence in target relevance increases. A single toxic compound producing that phenotype is weaker evidence.|target validation requires converging causal evidence|Do not treat expression correlation as proof that inhibiting the protein will be beneficial.|Target validation reduces the risk of optimizing compounds against an irrelevant biological mechanism.
Assay development|Assay development builds a measurement system that can distinguish a desired biological response from noise and interference.|Controls establish signal windows and reveal artifacts such as fluorescence interference, aggregation or nonspecific toxicity. A sensitive readout is not necessarily a selective measure of the target process.|A fluorescent compound appears to inhibit an enzyme in a fluorescence assay but shows no inhibition in an orthogonal absorbance assay. The discrepancy suggests a readout artifact that needs investigation.|measured signal = biological response + assay background + interference|Do not identify every reduction in assay signal as target inhibition.|Well-controlled assays support reproducible screening and mechanism studies.
Hit discovery and screening|Hit discovery identifies starting compounds that reproducibly affect a validated assay and survive artifact checks.|Primary screening results require confirmation, concentration-response testing and suitable counter-screens. A single high-concentration response is not yet a validated lead.|A compound active in one screening well is retested from fresh material across concentrations. If its response disappears with a detergent control or fails orthogonal testing, nonspecific interference becomes a plausible explanation.|primary hit -> confirmation -> counterscreen -> qualified starting point|Do not equate a positive primary well with a selective, developable drug candidate.|Hit triage prioritizes credible chemical matter for further optimization.
Hit-to-lead optimization|Hit-to-lead optimization improves a starting series across potency, selectivity and physicochemical properties while retaining interpretable structure-activity relationships.|Changing one substituent can improve target affinity but worsen solubility or metabolic stability. Compound selection therefore requires multiple measured properties rather than a potency-only ranking.|An analogue gains tenfold biochemical potency but loses aqueous solubility and cellular activity. The result does not establish a better lead until exposure and assay behaviour are understood.|lead quality depends on a multiparameter profile|Do not assume that a lower IC50 automatically means a more useful lead.|Balanced analogue design builds series suitable for deeper pharmacology and exposure studies.
Candidate selection|Candidate selection compares integrated evidence for compounds proposed for formal development.|Potency, exposure, safety, formulation, synthetic accessibility and uncertainty all contribute. The best compound in one assay can be a poor development choice because of liabilities elsewhere.|Two leads have similar cellular potency; one has reproducible exposure and manageable formulation, while the other shows unstable exposure and a reactive-metabolite signal. Selection requires evaluating those differences rather than ranking assay potency alone.|candidate decision integrates efficacy potential, exposure, safety and manufacturability|Do not select a candidate from a single favourable endpoint while ignoring unresolved material risks.|Integrated candidate reviews support a defensible transition from research to development.
Absorption and distribution|Absorption determines entry into systemic circulation, while distribution describes movement between blood and tissues.|Ionization, permeability, binding and transport affect exposure. Plasma concentration does not automatically equal unbound concentration at a target site.|A highly protein-bound compound can have a high total plasma concentration but a much smaller unbound fraction. Comparing total concentration directly with a biochemical potency value can therefore misrepresent available exposure.|unbound concentration = unbound fraction x total concentration|Do not assume high total plasma concentration guarantees adequate unbound tissue exposure.|Absorption and distribution studies connect chemical properties with pharmacologically relevant exposure.
Clearance and half-life|Clearance describes elimination capacity as a volume per time, while half-life describes the time for a defined concentration decline.|For a linear one-compartment model, elimination half-life depends on both clearance and apparent distribution volume. Different distribution behaviour can change half-life even at the same clearance.|With apparent volume 20 L and clearance 2 L/h, t-half = 0.693 x 20/2 = 6.93 h in the stated model. Doubling volume at unchanged clearance doubles that half-life.|t-half = ln(2) x V/CL in a linear one-compartment model|Half-life is not clearance expressed in different units and is not always concentration-independent.|Clearance-volume relationships help interpret exposure profiles without assigning clinical doses.
PK/PD modelling|PK/PD modelling links concentration-time profiles to measured pharmacological responses.|A response may lag behind plasma concentration because of distribution or biological turnover. Model structure and identifiability determine whether fitted parameters have a defensible interpretation.|If an effect peaks after plasma concentration peaks, a direct instantaneous concentration-effect model may fail. An effect-compartment or turnover model can be tested against the time course rather than forcing both peaks to coincide.|PK describes exposure; PD describes response linked to exposure|Do not treat a fitted concentration-effect relationship as proof of a unique mechanism without checking alternative models.|PK/PD models organize exposure-response evidence during compound evaluation.
QSAR|Quantitative structure-activity relationships use structural descriptors to model a defined biological or physicochemical endpoint.|A useful model requires appropriate chemical coverage, validation and an applicability domain. Closely related duplicates split across training and test sets can inflate apparent performance.|A model trained on one scaffold family predicts its close analogues well but fails on a distant scaffold. That failure can indicate use outside the applicability domain rather than random assay noise alone.|activity estimate = function of validated molecular descriptors|Do not report training correlation as independent predictive accuracy or infer causal mechanism from descriptor coefficients alone.|QSAR supports hypothesis generation and prioritization within a defensible chemical domain.
Dosage forms|Dosage forms organize a drug substance and excipients into a delivery system with defined release and stability characteristics.|Tablets, solutions and controlled-release systems impose different constraints. Dissolution, disintegration and absorption are related but distinct processes.|A tablet may disintegrate into particles rapidly but dissolve its poorly soluble active ingredient slowly. A disintegration result alone therefore cannot establish complete drug dissolution.|disintegration breaks dosage-form structure; dissolution transfers solute into solution|Do not substitute a disintegration time for a dissolution profile.|Dosage-form design connects material properties with release behaviour and product quality.
Drug delivery systems|Drug-delivery systems alter how a drug is released, transported or retained in a biological environment.|Carrier loading, release mechanism, stability and biodistribution must be evaluated separately. Encapsulation does not automatically establish target specificity or clinical benefit.|A polymer carrier can slow release in a buffer assay, but that result does not prove selective accumulation at a diseased tissue. Release and targeting require different measurements.|delivery performance combines release kinetics, transport and stability|Do not equate nanoscale particle size or encapsulation efficiency with demonstrated biological targeting.|Delivery research addresses exposure limitations through material and formulation design.
Stability and degradation|Drug stability describes changes in chemical or physical quality under specified storage and use conditions.|Hydrolysis, oxidation, photolysis and solid-state changes require different controls. A degradation model must match the observed mechanism and experimental conditions.|For a compound showing first-order loss under a defined condition, concentration falls exponentially. Protecting it from moisture may reduce a hydrolysis pathway, but not necessarily an independent light-driven degradation pathway.|ln(C/C0) = -kt for a validated first-order degradation model|Do not infer long-term stability from one unchanged assay result or extrapolate across a mechanism change.|Stability-indicating methods guide packaging, storage and formulation development.
Chiral resolution|Chiral resolution separates enantiomers using a chiral influence because enantiomers behave identically in many achiral environments.|A chiral stationary phase or diastereomeric derivative can create distinguishable interactions. R/S and E/Z notation|R/S descriptors specify tetrahedral stereocentre configuration, while E/Z descriptors specify alkene substituent relationships using priority rules.|Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priorities depend on atomic-number and tie-breaking rules. For an alkene, high-priority groups on opposite sides give E; applying a tetrahedral clockwise rule to the alkene would be inappropriate.|R/S uses a tetrahedral viewing rule; E/Z compares priorities across a double bond|R does not imply positive optical rotation, and E/Z is not assigned by choosing the larger-looking groups.|Unambiguous stereodescriptors communicate molecular configuration across drawings and names.
Stereoselective reactions|A stereoselective reaction forms one stereoisomer preferentially from a set of possible products.|Selectivity is a product ratio, while stereospecificity describes a mechanistic relationship between distinct starting stereoisomers and products. A reaction can be selective without both terms applying identically.|Hydrogenation from one face of a suitable constrained substrate may give a major diastereomer. Measuring the diastereomer ratio establishes selectivity; it does not by itself assign every mechanistic step.|stereoselectivity is quantified by stereoisomer product ratios|Do not use stereoselective and stereospecific as interchangeable words.|Stereochemical control improves the efficiency of synthesis when product configuration affects properties.
Aromatic substitution|Aromatic substitution replaces a substituent or hydrogen on an aromatic ring through a mechanism that depends on reagents and ring activation.|Electrophilic aromatic substitution commonly restores aromaticity after addition. Nucleophilic aromatic substitution can require electron-withdrawing activation and an appropriate leaving group in a different pathway.|Nitration of benzene proceeds through electrophilic attack and loss of a proton to restore aromaticity. Neutralization|Neutralization is proton transfer between an acid and a base, often producing water and a salt in aqueous strong-acid/strong-base reactions.|For strong acids and bases, spectator ions do not appear in the net ionic equation.
